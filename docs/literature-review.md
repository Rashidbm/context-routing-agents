# Literature review: context routing for AI agents

_Prepared 19 October 2026. The team shared its project idea on 11 September 2026; [Sentry](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994) was published on 2 October 2026. We study it as later research evidence for design and evaluation, not as the origin of the team's idea. Read Sections 2–5 and the limitations in Section 6._

## The question we are studying

An agent can carry useful instructions and still use them at the wrong time. A lesson such as “broaden the search after repeated empty results” is conditional: it helps after a failed search, but can distract an agent that has already found a suitable answer. Our proposed **context router** observes the agent's current state, selects a reviewed lesson when its trigger applies, and records whether that intervention helped. The research question is whether this improves **new tasks**, compared with both no lesson and the same lesson placed in every prompt. This is a hypothesis for our project, not a guaranteed outcome.

## Central paper: Sentry

[Sentry: Learning to Recover from LLM Agent Failures at Test Time](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994) (Ji et al., October 2026) is the closest later research evidence we found for conditional failure lessons. It argues that failure recovery knowledge should be kept outside the agent's persistent context and exposed only after a matching failure is diagnosed. The authors show two problems with earlier approaches: task-level memories can be shown before the failure exists, while fixed runtime warnings do not accumulate verified lessons from earlier recoveries. In a controlled comparison, adding the whole frozen playbook to the agent's persistent context reduced WebShop reward from 0.364 to 0.296 and Mind2Web Replay completion from 0.651 to 0.599, even though triggered retrieval remained available. These are results in the paper's specific environments, not expected gains for our project. [Source: Sentry, Sections 2 and 5.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994).

Sentry watches a recent window of steps. Invalid actions go through a schema-based retry (“hard repair”). For progress failures such as loops, and reasoning or grounding failures, it produces a diagnosis and retrieves a bounded number of matching entries from an external recovery playbook (“soft repair”). Its entries include a failure type, labels, trigger pattern, and repair principle. After intervention, a verifier examines later steps and admits a new lesson only if it judges that the *local failure* was resolved. That verifier does not see the benchmark reward or final success label; the method's monitoring and retrieval rules stay fixed while the playbook grows. [Source: Sentry, Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994).

The evidence is substantial but bounded. Sentry reports results on WebShop, AppWorld, SWE-bench Lite, and Mind2Web Replay. It outperforms the strongest runtime-intervention comparator on each benchmark, averaging a 37% relative gain; against the strongest context-evolution comparator, it reports a 39% relative gain on the two benchmarks where both were evaluated. A separate held-out protocol initializes memory on 50 tasks and scores 50 disjoint tasks. In a paired analysis of 200 intervention points, local recovery rose from 33.5% without Sentry guidance to 60.5% with it. The authors also report that intervention and recovery judgments agreed with human annotators in 86.0% and 81.8% of sampled cases, respectively. [Source: Sentry, Sections 4–5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994).

We should not treat those numbers as universal. Sentry studied two backbone models, uses the same model family for agent, detector, and verifier, and notes that its playbook may collect redundant or overly specific entries. Its total token use was 1.54 times the base agent's across the reported benchmarks, and wall-clock time improved on some tasks but worsened on others. The Mind2Web **Replay** adaptation permits retry against recorded states; it is not an ordinary live-web benchmark. These limits are reasons to measure our own precision, failures, latency, and cost. [Source: Sentry, Sections 5.4–6 and Appendix A.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994).

**Our scope differs from a full Sentry replication.** Sentry already has a [public MIT-licensed repository](https://github.com/nuglifeleoji/Sentry). We will cite it and inspect it for prior art. Our first version should emphasize a small, inspectable developer tool: one adapter, observable trace events, explicit human approval of lessons, deterministic matching, and a reproducible comparison. Sentry's automatic detection, model-based verification, multiple failure categories, and four-benchmark study are useful references, not first-version requirements. If we later reuse code, we must follow its license and attribution; building our own simple implementation is also possible.

## Related research: what each paper contributes

| Work | Main mechanism | What it teaches us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Reflexion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366) (Shinn et al., 2023) | Turns task feedback into verbal reflections kept in episodic memory for later trials, without changing model weights. | A textual lesson can help an agent learn across attempts. It does not by itself solve when a failure-specific lesson should appear *mid-task*. |
| [Agentic Context Engineering (ACE)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04618) (Zhang et al., 2025/2026) | Evolves a structured playbook through generation, reflection, and curation; incremental updates preserve details. | Store lessons as structured, versioned entries. Its broader context evolution is a useful baseline, while our first version routes one approved recovery lesson at a specific step. |
| [ReasoningBank](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25140) (Ouyang et al., 2025/2026) | Distills strategies from successful and failed experience and retrieves relevant memory for a new task. | General strategy memory is useful, but task-start retrieval and failure-time routing answer different questions. Include “always available” as a comparator. |
| [Wink](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17037) (Nanda et al., 2026) | Monitors coding-agent trajectories and gives targeted course-correction guidance during execution. | Runtime observation and intervention matter. A fixed or freshly generated correction does not by itself demonstrate that a validated lesson transfers across future tasks. |

These papers are related, not interchangeable. Reflexion, ACE, and ReasoningBank primarily show ways to **retain experience**. Wink shows how to **intervene while an agent runs**. Sentry combines failure-time intervention with reusable, verified recovery lessons. Our project puts a developer-facing review, preview, and routing workflow around that class of idea; it does not claim those concepts are unique. The team should read the cited papers for their original methods and evaluation conditions before making comparative claims. [Sentry's related-work discussion](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994) provides a map, but the linked papers are the primary sources for each row.

## Existing developer tools and positioning

[Mirrors](https://mirrors.dev/) already describes a product very close to the original project idea: inspect agent traces, write guidance for a recurring issue, preview the sessions it would reach, route context mid-conversation, and compare outcomes. Its site also describes changing tool descriptions, input schemas, or tool results. Treat Mirrors as a product reference and avoid claiming that trace-to-lesson routing is a new invention. Its public site describes product behavior; it does not establish the licensing status of every component, so we should not make a blanket “closed source” claim without checking terms or repositories. [Source: Mirrors product site](https://mirrors.dev/).

[Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/docs) and [Arize Phoenix](https://arize.com/docs/phoenix/) are open-source tools for tracing, datasets, experiments, and evaluation. Their documented core value is observing and comparing agent behavior; they are potential integrations or alternatives for our tracing UI, rather than proof that we need to build another generic observability platform. We should keep our first dashboard small and spend effort on the distinctive loop: **reviewed lesson → precise runtime match → logged intervention → measured outcome**. [Sources: Langfuse docs](https://langfuse.com/docs), [Phoenix docs](https://arize.com/docs/phoenix/).

The useful open-source contribution is a Mirrors-like workflow that developers can inspect and run themselves: understandable API, easy local setup, trace and lesson review UI, examples, tests, and honest results. GitHub stars may follow usefulness, but they are not an evaluation metric. We can describe this as a student-built, open-source implementation and learning project, while being clear that Sentry and other adjacent projects also publish source.

## What our five-person team should build and test

1. **One reproducible agent scenario.** Use a small tool-using agent with a failure that is observable, such as repeating the same empty-result search. Include similar cases where a retry is valid, so routing can make mistakes. Document the task, tools, model, budget, and success check before tuning lessons.
2. **A minimal trace contract.** Record task and run IDs, ordered tool calls and outputs, observable agent actions, final outcome, model version, and timing or token data. Do not require hidden chain-of-thought. Redact secrets and personal data before persistence. Our [architecture contract](architecture.md) defines the first JSON schema.
3. **Pattern and lesson workflow.** Group obvious recurring failures from traces first (for example, repeated identical calls after empty results); show the linked runs in a small dashboard and propose a draft lesson. Its record has a trigger, guidance text, evidence run IDs, version, and draft/approved/rejected status. Before approval, preview which historical events would match and show counterexamples. This deliberately simplifies Sentry's automatic verifier; approval and preview are guardrails, not evidence of improved task outcomes.
4. **A transparent router.** Match approved lessons against the current observable event before the next agent call. Log both matches and non-matches with reason and lesson version. Start with deterministic predicates; add semantic matching only if false negatives justify it.
5. **A held-out evaluation.** Write lessons and tune triggers on development tasks. Freeze the lesson and router settings, then run the same disjoint evaluation tasks under three conditions: base agent, lesson always in the prompt, and routed lesson. Keep model, tools, task order policy, and budgets comparable. Score task success, repeat failures, routing precision/recall on labeled steps, agent steps, tokens, latency, and cost. Report sample sizes and individual failures, not just a percentage. Historical trace replay can measure which steps *would have matched*; only rerunning the agent can measure changed behavior. See the [evaluation plan](evaluation.md).

The first demo should show one repeated pattern across traces, the draft lesson and its preview, the approval step, one new task where the router injects it, one negative case where it stays silent, and a table of all three evaluation conditions. If routing fails to beat the always-on prompt, that is still a credible result: the traces should tell us whether the trigger, lesson, or scenario needs revision.

## Reading order for contributors

- **Everyone:** Sentry [Sections 1–3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994) for the problem and mechanism, then [Sections 4–6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994) for evaluation and limits.
- **Trace and integration owner:** Sentry [Section 3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994) and the [public integration example](https://github.com/nuglifeleoji/Sentry).
- **Lesson and review owner:** [ACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04618) for structured playbooks, plus Sentry [Section 3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994) for verifying recoveries.
- **Router owner:** Sentry [Section 3.2 and retrieval ablations](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994) for bounded, failure-specific matching; compare the workflow described by [Mirrors](https://mirrors.dev/).
- **Evaluation owner and team lead:** Sentry [Sections 4–5 and Appendix A.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02994) for matched baselines, disjoint task sets, and honest overhead reporting; use our smaller [evaluation plan](evaluation.md) as the executable protocol.
